The Seedance 2.0 Film System

Four blind generations, one film that stitches with no post. The producer layer behind multi-scene AI film, plus the free Claude skill that runs it for you.

      One good clip is easy now. Four clips that play as one film is not. Same face, same place, same grade, a story that climbs, and an ending that lands. No compositing, no grade matching, no sound pass to rescue it.

      Most AI films fall apart at the second scene. Not because the renderer is weak. Because the model generating scene four has never seen scenes one to three. It has no memory, no context, and no way to infer anything you did not write down in front of it.

      Every rule on this page follows from that one fact.

      You stop generating pretty clips that refuse to cut together. You stop watching your character turn into someone else at the seam. You stop finding out halfway through that the one thing your concept depends on does not render.

      01 · Why it breaksEvery generation is a cold start

      The model has never seen your other clips. It does not know this is scene three. It cannot tell that a shirt was dry ten seconds ago. It has nothing except the reference images and the words in this one prompt.

      That produces two consequences. Almost everyone gets the second one wrong.

      Consequence one: nothing can be referenced. "The same corridor" builds a new corridor from scratch. "She still has the torch" invents a torch. "GENERATION 3 OF 4" is noise that implies a context which does not exist. And cumulative timecodes are the quiet killer. Writing SHOT 1 (00:45-00:47) tells a model with no memory to render the middle of something it cannot see.

      Consequence two: everything must be described, but everything can still change. The instinctive fix for consequence one is to freeze the world so nothing needs remembering. Same clothes, same place, same light, four times. That instinct kills the film. A story where nothing accumulates has no escalation, and viewers feel it straight away as "the scenes do not continue" or "nothing is happening".

      The discipline is describe, don't infer. The world changes freely between scenes. Each prompt describes its own state completely, from scratch, in the present tense, as if the audience and the model are both seeing it for the first time.

      Depends on memory (breaks)Describes the state (renders)
      his shirt is now soaked and the maze is half destroyedhe wears an off-white cotton tee, soaked through and clinging to his chest and shoulders, the hem hanging heavy and dripping. Two of the three clotheslines are down, their sheets lying flat in the standing water; the third hangs intact.

      Same information. One needs a memory the model does not have. The other renders.

      Each law exists because breaking it produced a specific failure I watched happen.

      #LawWhat it prevents

      The math

      A 60 second narrative short the traditional way means crew, cast, location, permits, a shoot day and a post schedule. Thousands, and weeks.

      This way: one operator, an afternoon of architecture, then four generations. The architecture is the expensive part and it is all text. A bad story decision costs a retype instead of a reshoot. You can throw away three concepts before lunch, and that is the real leverage.

      05 · The workflowThe workflow, step by step

      Nine steps in three phases. Plan the film, lock the world, then render. The skill runs all nine in order. Here is what each one does and what you should be holding at the end of it.

      Phase one · Plan the film
      01
      Plan

      Style, spec and the spine gate

      Pick the visual style first: photoreal, cinematic live action, 2D anime, 3D, stop motion, found footage. It governs the concept, the failure modes, the reference images and the vocabulary in every brief. Never mix styles, or the model splits the difference and gives you neither.

      Then lock the spec: total duration, number of generations, length per generation, aspect ratio.

      Then run the spine gate. Five plain sentences, all five required:

      The spine gate
      WANT:        somebody wants something specific and concrete
OBSTACLE:    something or someone stands directly in the way
DECISION:    the character makes an active choice, not a discovery
CONSEQUENCE: the choice produces a physical result
CHANGE:      something is different at the end than at the start

      Read it aloud like a story someone told you. "A boy goes up to a roof to get his shirt, a man comes up behind him and cuts him off from the only door, he unhooks a clothesline and holds it across the aisle at chest height, the man runs into it, and the boy walks out past him." That is a film.

      Compare: "A boy on a roof notices the laundry moving strangely and finds his shirt cut in half." That has a Want and a Consequence and nothing else. It is an image, not a story, and an audience experiences it as nothing happening.

      The Decision line is the one most often missing. A character who only watches or reacts is not driving a film.

      You now haveA style, a locked spec and five sentences that pass the gate.
      02
      Plan

      The four originality passes

      Pass 1: name the obvious version, then ban it. Write it down before designing anything, because unnamed defaults get built by accident. An anime short defaults to a schoolgirl on a rooftop with wind and blossom. A monster film defaults to a creature stalking someone through a dark house. Name yours and forbid it.

      Pass 2: move the camera to the wrong place. The person who arrives eleven minutes late. The person next to the event: the cleaner, the dispatcher, the kid in the back seat. The surface the event happened on. One caution: that person still needs their own want, obstacle and decision, or a clever framing becomes a non-film.

      Pass 3: find the physical mechanism. Ask what physical fact about this world could decide the outcome. A rooftop hung with clotheslines at adult chest height, in a film about a child and an adult, holds its own ending: the child goes under without thinking, the adult does not. Plant it early with no emphasis and the ending feels inevitable instead of convenient. A film with a good mechanism does not need a twist.

      Pass 4: decide what stays unseen. Withhold the antagonist's motive, the moment of impact, what is in the bag. Never withhold the spine itself.

      You now haveThe default version named and banned, a physical mechanism planted, and a decision about what the audience never sees.
      03
      Plan

      The pre-flight risk pass

      Before designing a single shot, list every element the concept needs and triage each one.

      • SAFE generates reliably. Build the film out of these.
      • CONVERT will not generate well as imagined. Redesign the beat so it delivers the same meaning another way.
      • HIDE must exist but must not be seen clearly. Make it partial, brief, blocked or under-lit.
      ElementTriageDecision
      Two adults fighting in clear viewCONVERTOne contact, not an exchange. Sustained fight choreography is where generation breaks.
      The moment of impactHIDECut on the contact frame. The impact is an edit, not an image.
      The antagonist's face across four clipsSAFE, with a conditionHe gets his own reference image. A silhouette costs clarity and drifts anyway.
      Laundry blowing in windCONVERTThe air is dead and the sheets are water heavy. Stated in every constants block.
      Blood and injuryHIDEHe goes down and stays down. The fall carries it.

      Look at what the triage did. It turned an unbuildable brief into a buildable film, and made it better. That is the normal outcome. Constraints found early become the concept. Constraints found late become troubleshooting.

      Never reliable: readable text, logos, clocks with correct hands, readable screens, consistent reflections, and anything the audience can count. A wall of tally marks works because nobody counts them. A display reading "07" does not.

      You now haveEvery element of the concept marked SAFE, CONVERT or HIDE, with the redesigns written down.
      Phase two · Lock the world
      04
      Build

      The energy ladder

      Every scene gets a declared energy level, and the ladder climbs. A viewer should be able to tell which scene they are watching from its pulse alone, with the sound off.

      LevelCameraCut rateMotion in framePerformance
      1 StillLocked offOne shot per 3 to 4sOne element movesNeutral
      2 ObservedSlow deliberate movesOne shot per 3sSubject moves, world stillContained
      3 ActiveHandheld with weightOne shot per 2.5sSubject and one world elementUrgency
      4 PressedReal instability, whip pansOne shot per 2sSeveral elements displacedFull commitment
      5 BrokenViolent displacementOne shot per 1 to 1.5sThe environment is wreckedMaximum, then stillness

      The ladder must climb, and it may drop once after the peak. A 60 second film often runs 1, 3, 4, then 5 into 1. The drop to stillness is what makes an ending land.

      Energy is not a label. Writing "scene 3 is high energy" does nothing unless the prompt itself is different: camera behaviour, cut rate, how much moves in frame, and how hard the performance pushes. Same location, same light, same grade, four dimensions changed. That is what makes a stitched film feel like it is accelerating.

      Shot budget: roughly one shot per 2.5 to 3 seconds, never more than six in a 15 second generation. Cramming eight camera setups into fifteen seconds is a direct cause of unusable output. Fewer, longer shots are almost always the more reliable and the more filmic choice.

      You now haveAn energy level for every scene, climbing, with a shot budget per generation.
      05
      Build

      The constants block

      One block of text, pasted word for word at the top of every generation prompt. Identical wording is not a style preference. Two descriptions of the same thing produce two results, so "cold white torch" in one prompt and "harsh flashlight" in another are two different lights.

      The constants block template
      REFERENCE INPUTS: @image1 = [who/what, identity lock]. @image2 = [who/what,
  identity lock]. Both hold exactly as drawn in every shot.

[CHARACTER 1] (@image1): [age, build]. [Full wardrobe: every garment by colour,
  material, condition, how it is worn.] [Three identity anchors, named.]

STYLE: [style, technique, grain, texture, and the explicit list of what is
  not permitted: no CG, no bloom, no glow, no speed lines.]

LOCATION: [the space, its architecture, its geography. Where each feature sits
  relative to the others, by compass direction or a clear spatial relationship.
  Every surface and its condition.]

LIGHT: [ONE source, its position, colour and hardness. Which parts of the space
  are lit and which are dark. The explicit instruction that everything the
  source does not reach is genuinely black: no fill, no ambient lift.]

GRADE: [exact same words every time. Name the only saturated colours allowed.]
  Lens family: [one, held throughout].

MOTION RULE: [what does and does not move on its own, and the explicit statement
  that nothing moves unless something in frame moves it.]

Aspect ratio: [x]. Duration: [x] seconds.

      The geography line does more work than it looks like. Naming where the door sits relative to the light, and the light relative to the subject, is what lets four separate generations feel like one continuous space. Without it, each generation invents its own floor plan.

      You now haveOne constants block, final, that you will paste unchanged into every prompt.
      06
      Build

      The state ledger

      The constants block holds everything that never changes. The state ledger holds everything that does, written so it never depends on memory. One row per scene, and every cell is a complete description that stands on its own.

      SceneSubject stateLocation stateLight state
      1Off-white tee, dry and clean, collar stretched. Sandals dry.Three clotheslines strung intact, sheets hanging straight. Concrete wet with shallow standing water.Sodium lamp burning steadily, full amber.
      2Off-white tee damp across the shoulders and hem. Sandals wet, dark at the edges.Three lines intact. One sheet slipped a peg, hanging at an angle.Sodium lamp burning steadily, full amber.
      3Tee soaked through and clinging, hem dripping. Grit across one hip. Both palms red and raw.Two lines strung; the third lies flat in the standing water with its sheets.Sodium lamp flickering, dropping to half and recovering.
      4Tee soaked, one shoulder seam pulled open, forearms grazed.One line pulled tight across the aisle at chest height, bare. Fallen sheets in the water.Sodium lamp steady again, full amber.

      How to check a row. Read the cell aloud with no other context. If it makes complete sense to someone who has seen nothing else, it is right. If it contains a comparison, a change verb or a time reference, it is wrong.

      This is where escalation lives. It is where a film gets more dynamic scene by scene, and it costs nothing to render because each state is just a description.

      You now haveA ledger row for every scene where each cell reads correctly on its own.
      07
      Build

      Reference images

      Every character who appears in more than one generation gets their own numbered reference. No exceptions. Hiding a secondary character in silhouette to dodge drift fails twice: the film becomes hard to read, and the drift happens anyway because build, clothing and props drift too.

      • Neutral light, neutral expression, always. A dramatically lit reference bakes its lighting into every generation. Flat and boring is correct, even though it looks dull on its own.
      • Three distinct identity anchors per character: a scar through an eyebrow, a tape patch in a named position, a saturated colour accessory, a hair behaviour, a flaw in a garment. Match them to shot size. In a wide or near silhouette only high contrast shape and colour survive.
      • Full body or three quarter, so briefs can call any shot size later. A head and shoulders reference makes every wide shot a guess.
      • Every image must look like it came from the same production. Hold background tone, light direction, line weight, palette and crop identical, written as the same sentences rather than paraphrased.
      • Anchors that rhyme across characters are free. If the protagonist carries the only red object and the antagonist the second, the eye tracks both and the film gains a relationship nobody has to explain.

      Typical counts: 15 to 20 second films need one or two images, 30 to 45 seconds need two to four, and a 60 second film with two characters and two locations may need four or five.

      Do not add a reference for progressive wear like wet clothing or dirt. References hold identity. The state ledger holds condition.

      You now haveA numbered set of neutral, matching reference images, generated and saved before any video render.
      Phase three · Render
      08
      Render

      The scene map

      The plan you work from, and the only place film time is allowed to appear anywhere in the whole deliverable.

      SceneFilm timeLengthStory beatEnergyOpens onCloses onImages
      100:00-00:1515sHe goes for his shirt; a man comes through the door1 StillLocked wide, doorwayThe door opening behind him@image1, @image2
      200:15-00:3015sThe man comes up the aisle; he runs; he is cornered3 ActiveGround macro, bootsPinned at the parapet@image1, @image2
      300:30-00:4515sCaught, breaks free, unhooks the wire4 PressedMacro, tank legsWire coming loose in his hands@image1, @image2
      400:45-01:0015sBrace, charge, impact, he walks out5 then 1Macro, taut wireThe door swinging shut@image1, @image2

      Then throw the film time away. It never enters a prompt.

      The seam rule: every scene opens on a different shot from the previous close. Different framing, different movement, different distance. Two independent generations never match a moving frame, and drift reads as a fault. A cut to a new angle is invisible because the eye expects cuts. Vary the seam type too: a detail macro, a reverse angle, an environment plate, a point of view, the space next door, a jump in scale.

      You now haveA scene map with a beat, an energy level, an opening and a closing shot for every generation.
      09
      Render

      The generation prompts

      Each generation gets its own brief containing: its own duration and aspect ratio, its reference images by number, the constants block word for word, this scene's ledger row, this scene's energy level and what that means for camera, cut rate and motion, the story beat in one sentence, the opening shot and how it differs from the previous close, the shot budget, and the instruction that every timecode starts at 00:00.

      Strip from every prompt body: generation numbers, any instruction addressed to you instead of the model, score or music direction, any reference to another scene, any post production fallback, any take count.

      Sound deserves its own note. Seedance 2.0 generates audio in the same pass, so describe only sound with a visible cause in frame: footsteps on wet concrete, a door slamming, a line of dialogue. Score direction inside a prompt either does nothing or bleeds into the picture. Music belongs in a separate audio plan you add in the edit.

      06 · Quality gateThe banned words and the self-check

      Inside a prompt body these words almost always signal an inference the model cannot make. When one appears, rewrite the sentence as a complete present tense description.

      TypeWords
      Change verbsnow, still, again, already, remains, continues, returns, becomes, no longer
      Comparativesmore, less, further, darker, wetter, worse, increasingly
      Referencessame as, like before, as established, from the previous, earlier
      Sequence markersscene 2, generation 3, of 4, continuing, next, following
      Meta instructionspaste this, the user should, in the edit, in post

      One legitimate exception: "the same X across every shot" inside a single prompt is an identity lock, and it is correct.

      Run this on every prompt before you render. It is mechanical on purpose. These failures survive being understood, which is why they must be checked instead of trusted.

      The final test: drop every clip onto a timeline end to end with no adjustment. Does it play as one film? If the honest answer is no, the plan is not finished.

      07 · When it breaksTroubleshooting

      ProblemFix
      Clips will not cut togetherThe constants block is not word identical. Paste, never paraphrase.
      The face changes between scenesA missing reference, or anchors too fine for the shot size. Use shape and colour for wide shots.
      The film feels flatYou froze the world. Build a state ledger and let it degrade.
      "Nothing happens"No spine. Go back to the five sentences and find the Decision.
      A scene renders the wrong momentCumulative timecodes leaked in. Every prompt restarts at 00:00.
      Shots blended or droppedOver budget. Cap at one shot per 2.5 to 3 seconds.
      A visible seam between scenesTwo similar angles. Make the next opening shot as different as possible.
      The location changes shapeThe geography line is missing from the constants block. Name what sits where.
      Late scenes drift the mostFront load identity. Move to hands, backs and silhouettes as the film goes on.

      08 · AvoidWhat not to do

      • Don't put film time in a prompt. If you write SHOT 1 (00:45-00:47), the prompt is broken.
      • Don't freeze the world to solve the memory problem. Describe the change fully instead.
      • Don't deliver atmosphere as a film. The spine gate is not optional.
      • Don't flatten the energy across scenes. The ladder climbs, always.
      • Don't plan around post production. If a shot only works with compositing, redesign the shot.
      • Don't overload a generation. Six shots maximum in 15 seconds.
      • Don't hide a recurring character to avoid drift. Give them a reference.
      • Don't run more than six generations. Past that, identity drift dominates. Restructure instead.
      • Don't use hype words in prompts. No stunning, breathtaking, epic, or cinematic as filler.
